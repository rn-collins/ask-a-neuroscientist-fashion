# Seven recording-ready vertical scripts

Every script closes with an evidence label and the full exhibition URL. Target 45–60 seconds. Captions required.

## 01 — The coat that supposedly changes attention

“You may have heard that wearing a lab coat makes you more attentive. The original 2012 experiment did report fewer errors on certain Stroop trials. But it did not measure intelligence, and it did not look at the brain. In 2019, a preregistered direct replication failed to recover that effect and concluded that, if it exists, it was too small for the original design to detect reliably. So the scientific label is not ‘life hack.’ It is ‘disputed finding.’ Read the claim, replication and boundary in The Dressed Nervous System.”

On-screen source: Adam & Galinsky (2012), doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2012.02.008; Burns et al. (2019), doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2018.10.001.

## 02 — A garment is not a brain scan

“A study can be psychological without being neurological. The famous enclothed-cognition experiments used behavioral attention tasks. They did not use brain imaging. That means ‘participants made fewer errors under this condition’ cannot become ‘the coat rewired their brain.’ Before sharing a neuroscience-sounding fashion claim, ask three things: what was measured, whether it replicated, and where the evidence stops. In this case: behavior was measured, the key Stroop effect did not directly replicate, and no neural mechanism was demonstrated.”

On-screen label: MEASURED BEHAVIOR ≠ MEASURED BRAIN.

## 03 — The replication people omit

“Five researchers repeated the famous lab-coat Stroop study under preregistration: Devin Burns, Elizabeth Fox, Michael Greenstein, Gayla Olbricht and DeMaris Montgomery. They did not recover the effect. Their design could detect other predicted effects as small as seven milliseconds, so the result was not dismissed as a broken task. A null replication does not prove clothing never matters. It does mean the viral claim no longer fits without a warning label.”

On-screen source: Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 83 (2019), 150–156.

## 04 — The corset test

“Look at this 1891 Maison Léoty corset. Even before cognition, it can shape posture, restrict movement, press against skin and produce a learned silhouette. Those pathways are not imaginary—but they are not interchangeable. A garment can change movement without improving attention. It can signal status without changing IQ. It can feel protective without proving a neural mechanism. ‘Clothes affect us’ is the beginning of the question, not the answer.”

Object credit: The Met, C.I.45.27a, b. Public Domain / CC0.

## 05 — A uniform changes the room first

“Sometimes the fastest clothing effect happens in somebody else. In a 2014 experiment, men assigned to business-suit clothing or sweatpants entered a negotiation. Researchers reported differences in behavior and physiology for wearers, plus vigilance-related physiological responses in their partners. This does not prove suits universally create power. It shows that clothing is a social signal. The room reads the garment—and the room’s response becomes part of the wearer’s experience.”

On-screen source: Kraus & Mendes (2014), doi:10.1037/xge0000023.

## 06 — What a meta-analysis cannot promise

“A later meta-analysis found evidence consistent with some enclothed-cognition effects, while the earliest studies showed weaker replicability. That is not a blank check for every fashion claim. Meta-analysis pools studies with different garments, meanings, tasks and outcomes. It can estimate a pattern. It cannot promise that your blazer, shoes or color choice will produce the same effect tomorrow. The honest label is ‘qualified,’ not ‘proven for everyone.’”

On-screen source: Horton, Adam & Galinsky, doi:10.1177/01461672231182478.

## 07 — Fit the sentence

“Try this before repeating a fashion-and-brain claim. First: name the garment. Second: name the manipulation—wearing it, seeing it, feeling it, or being treated differently because of it. Third: name the outcome that was actually measured. Fourth: check replication. Fifth: state what the study cannot prove. If your sentence survives all five fittings, wear it. If not, alter the claim—not the evidence.”
