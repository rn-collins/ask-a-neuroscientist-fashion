# AAN-F-007 — What Do Uniforms Do to the Wearer—and the Observer?

Status: recording-ready editorial master; RN performance, owner approval and specialist review remain human gates. Target: 12–14 minute film / 15–17 minute audio edition.

## Cold open — before the person speaks

VISUAL: The Met uniform 1979.152.54a–h appears as separate parts, then as a coordinated whole. Slow crops: collar, metal thread, sash, hat. On-screen credit remains visible.

RN: “Before the wearer says a word, the uniform has already been read. Occupation. Rank. Belonging. Authority. Safety. Threat. Some of those readings may coordinate a room. None of them gives us the whole person.”

AUDIO DESCRIPTION: “A dark nineteenth-century coat stands beside a white waistcoat and blue sash. A peaked hat completes the set.”

## Act I — the observer

RN: “Dress is part of person perception. Hester and Hehman’s review places it beside face, body, setting and perceiver characteristics. The important word is *perceiver*. Uniform meaning is not trapped inside cloth. It is made with context, culture, memory and power.”

ON SCREEN: SIGNAL ≠ PROOF.

RN: “Workplace research likewise finds that clothing can shape attributions of warmth and competence. An attribution is a judgment. It is not a credential check, a record of conduct or access to someone’s mind.”

SOURCE CARDS: U01, U02.

## Act II — the wearer

RN: “Can putting on a role change attention or action? Sometimes, in narrow experiments. Canadian university students wore a police-style uniform, mechanic’s clothing or other conditions. Across reaction-time tasks, the police-style uniform was associated with attention toward targets represented as low status by hoodies. The researchers did not find attention biased toward Black faces.”

RN: “That result is specific: students, a costume-like role cue, pictures, reaction time and one cultural setting. It is not evidence that police uniforms explain police conduct.”

RN: “Another study placed people in military, Red Cross or civilian clothing during an unusual laboratory moral-choice task involving mild shocks. Differences appeared. But a laboratory role is not a profession, and a protected experiment is not war, aid work or public service.”

SOURCE CARDS: U03, U04.

## Act III — the institution

VISUAL: Met military uniform 84440, then the empty space around it.

RN: “A uniform’s force comes partly from what surrounds it: who may wear it, who can discipline the wearer, what access it grants, what training it implies, and what histories the institution carries.”

RN: “In a UK hospital study, a shift toward one corporate uniform altered how staff described professional boundaries and status. That does not prescribe a universal hospital wardrobe. It shows that standardization can redistribute recognition.”

PROMPT ON SCREEN: Who chose it? Who must wear it? Who maintains it? Who is protected? Who is made visible?

SOURCE CARD: U05.

## Act IV — the brain-image trap

RN: “One small fMRI study showed people images of themselves and others in professional uniform and casual clothing while they judged adjectives. The authors reported activity across distributed self-, social- and affect-related systems. There is no uniform center. The scan does not prove the garment created professional pride, and it cannot tell us what every uniform means.”

SOURCE CARD: U06.

## Act V — fashion reads the system

VISUAL: The Met ensemble 2009.20a–j. Separate silhouette from context in three frames.

RN: “Uniforms are among fashion’s clearest demonstrations that design organizes social legibility. Cut, repetition and insignia can make a role readable at distance. They can also compress a person into a category, erase difference, or make institutional power look natural.”

RN: “A serious design brief therefore includes fit across bodies, sensory load, religious practice, gender expression, laundering, replacement, climate, safety, customization, discipline and refusal. A beautiful uniform can still fail the people required to wear it.”

## Act VI — map one encounter

SCREEN RECORD: Uniform Encounter Map, completed with a hypothetical historical collection object. No personal data.

RN: “Separate what was visible from what you inferred. Then separate inference from what you independently knew. Finally, name the history shaping the encounter. The gap is not a mistake. The gap is where responsible perception begins.”

## Close

RN: “So what does a uniform do? It can help a group coordinate. It can make a role salient. It can change how a wearer or observer approaches one situation. But it cannot certify character, competence, intent, safety or belonging. A uniform is a designed social signal carrying institutional history—not a switch for a human mind.”

END CARD: Ask what the garment signals. Then ask who gave the signal power.
