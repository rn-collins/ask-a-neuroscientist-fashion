## 1 — The invitation says one event

**Hook:** “The invitation says one event. Your nervous system gets six.”  
“A Fashion Week day can mean an early call, a queue, a loud room, bright light, hours of standing and a late edit. There is no special Fashion Week circuit. The useful neuroscience is to separate the conditions: sleep opportunity, timed light, sound, crowd movement, social evaluation and physical fatigue. Then attach the limit: studies of lab sleep loss or occupational noise do not measure your show. They tell us what might matter—not what happened to everyone.”  
**Screen:** F01–F09.  
**Next watch:** “Next: why can one fabric feel calming and another unbearable?”

## 2 — Fashion Week tired is not a diagnosis

“Short sleep can increase attentional lapses, but one early call is not the same as 24 hours awake in a laboratory. Record the schedule honestly: sleep opportunity, waking time and recovery. Do not turn tiredness into a glamorous badge—or a medical claim. The fitted sentence is: compressed schedules can reduce sleep opportunity, and sleep loss can make sustained attention less reliable.”  
**Screen:** F01; FW-S01/S02.  
**Next watch:** “The full event stack is in the exhibition.”

## 3 — A runway spotlight is not a dose

“Light can change alertness and circadian timing. But the effect depends on timing, spectrum, intensity, duration and the person. A photo that looks bright cannot tell you anyone’s retinal exposure. So no: we cannot diagnose circadian disruption from runway footage. We can ask better questions about late schedules, screen use, darkness and sleep opportunity.”  
**Screen:** F02; FW-S03.  
**Next watch:** “Next: the sound boundary.”

## 4 — Music is not automatically overload

“Noise research shows that sound can alter arousal and interfere with some tasks. But a soundtrack is not a diagnosis. Meaning, duration, predictability, control and individual sensitivity matter. Unless a show’s level and exposure were measured, the correct line is: sound may contribute to load—not: this show harmed everyone’s nervous system.”  
**Screen:** F03; FW-S04/S05.  
**Next watch:** “See the official 360-degree field object in the full exhibition.”

## 5 — What a crowd changes

“Crowding is more than a head count. In controlled field research, denser and slower pedestrian movement was associated with more arousal and perceived stress. Fashion Week queues and backstage rooms may share some conditions, but they are not the same experiment. Ask: could you move, predict, pause or leave? Control changes the lived scene.”  
**Screen:** F04/F05; FW-S06/S07.  
**Next watch:** “Next: why being seen can raise the stakes.”

## 6 — The social room

“In laboratories, social-evaluation tasks can produce cortisol and subjective stress. Fashion Week is full of looking—casting, cameras, seating, critique—but it is not the Trier Social Stress Test. Excitement, vigilance and distress are different. The research gives us a plausible mechanism and better interview questions, not permission to diagnose a room.”  
**Screen:** F06; FW-S08.  
**Next watch:** “Open the field mapper to record one situated experience.”

## 7 — Recovery is not optimization theater

“After a long event day, recovery does not need a readiness score. Notice the conditions: reduced sleep opportunity, standing, pressure, sound, rapid transitions. Then notice what helped: sitting, food, water, quieter time, changing shoes, fewer commitments, sleep. That is a field note—not treatment. If symptoms are persistent or severe, a website tool is not the right endpoint.”
