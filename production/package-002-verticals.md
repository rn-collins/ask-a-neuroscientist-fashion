# Package 002 — Seven vertical scripts

Each is 35–55 seconds. Preserve the evidence label on screen. End every cut with the exhibition or next-watch bridge.

## 01 — Armor is not a mechanism

“This outfit is my armor” can be completely true as an account of experience—and still not tell us why it works. The fabric may change pressure and movement. The outfit may remind you of a practiced role. Other people may treat you differently. Or dressing may start a familiar ritual. A feeling is evidence that something happened. It is not proof of one universal confidence circuit. In the full exhibition, we separate the four pathways.

## 02 — Your clothes never stop touching you

Clothing is continuous sensory input: pressure, heat, texture, weight and movement. Material-touch research shows that tactile and proprioceptive information contributes to how surfaces feel. So yes, an outfit can change bodily experience. No, that does not mean every fitted jacket calms anxiety. The question is: what changed in your body—and did it feel like support or restriction?

## 03 — Maybe the room changed

Sometimes the outfit does not “give” you confidence. Other people give you more distance, attention or deference. Dress is part of person perception, and observers use it to infer role, identity and traits—often inaccurately. If the social feedback changes, your experience changes. The pathway may run through the room.

## 04 — The ritual pathway

You wear the same boots before every difficult meeting. They are not storing courage. But they may be part of a learned sequence: get dressed, check the mirror, breathe, leave, act. Repetition can reduce decisions and retrieve memories of prior action. Test the pattern across several real occasions before turning one good day into a rule.

## 05 — When armor becomes a cage

An outfit can make you more socially protected while making your body less comfortable. It can restrict breath, aggravate pain, demand masking or turn visibility into surveillance. The right question is not only “Do I feel confident?” Ask: What does this help me do, what does my body tolerate, and what am I required to hide?

## 06 — A power suit cannot prove this

A strong silhouette can feel consequential. It cannot prove that your brain entered a confidence state, that cortisol fell, or that intelligence increased. Those claims require specific measures. The honest version is richer: clothing can change sensation, interpretation and other people’s behavior, and those changes may alter the encounter.

## 07 — One question before leaving

Before you leave in your armor outfit, ask: what do I predict will change—my body, my attention, my behavior or the room? Write it down. Afterward, record what actually happened and what it cost. Do that three times. You are building a personal observation, not prescribing a uniform for everyone.
