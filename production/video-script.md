## 0:35–1:30 — The object

**IMAGE**
Slow vertical study of Maison Léoty, *Corset*, 1891. The Met, C.I.45.27a, b. Public Domain.

**VOICEOVER**
Before we even get to cognition, look at what a garment can already do. This corset has structure. Pressure. Geometry. It organizes posture and movement. It helps produce a socially recognizable silhouette. Other people read that silhouette before the wearer says a word.

Clothing can affect a person without secretly turning fabric into a drug for the brain. It can constrain the body. Carry learned meaning. Change how a room responds. Those pathways are real questions. But they are different questions.

## 1:30–3:45 — The study everyone quotes

**RN TO CAMERA**
In 2012, psychologists Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky called their theory “enclothed cognition.” Their proposal was that clothing could influence psychological processes through two things happening together: the physical experience of wearing the clothing and the symbolic meaning attached to it.

The most famous experiment involved a white lab coat and a Stroop task. In a Stroop task, the meaning of a color word can conflict with the ink color you need to identify. It is a tightly controlled test of interference and selective attention.

The paper reported that participants wearing a lab coat made fewer errors on incongruent trials than participants wearing their own clothes. Later experiments varied whether a coat was described as a doctor’s coat or a painter’s coat, and whether participants wore it or merely saw it.

**ON SCREEN — EVIDENCE CARD C01**
REPORTED: task-specific attention differences in the 2012 experiments.
MEASURED: Stroop errors and a sustained-attention task.
NOT MEASURED: intelligence, IQ, creativity, confidence, long-term change, or the brain itself.
SOURCE: doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2012.02.008

**VOICEOVER**
That last line matters. There was no brain imaging. No evidence that a lab coat rewired anyone. “Fewer errors on particular trials in one experiment” cannot honestly become “clothes make you smarter.”

## 3:45–6:30 — The replication people omit

**CUT TO RN**
Then the result met a more difficult garment: replication.

In 2019, Devin Burns, Elizabeth Fox, Michael Greenstein, Gayla Olbricht and DeMaris Montgomery published a preregistered direct replication of the original Stroop experiment. Preregistration means the key questions and analysis were specified before seeing the outcome. It helps reduce the freedom to make a result look cleaner after the fact.

The replication did not recover the lab-coat Stroop effect.

The team did more than report a nonsignificant result. They used equivalence testing and a “small telescopes” framework to ask whether the effect, if it exists, was too small for the original design to detect reliably. Their answer was yes. They also successfully detected predicted sequential effects as small as seven milliseconds, which weakens the idea that their method simply could not find anything.

**ON SCREEN — EVIDENCE CARD C02**
RESULT: original Stroop effect not reproduced.
INTERPRETATION: any effect was too small for the original design to detect reliably.
DOES NOT PROVE: clothing can never affect cognition.
SOURCE: doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2018.10.001

**RN TO CAMERA**
A failed direct replication is not a magic eraser. It is a relabeling device. The famous lab-coat claim now needs the label “disputed,” not “scientific fact.”

## 6:30–8:15 — What the meta-analysis says

**VOICEOVER**
C. Blaine Horton Jr., Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky later analyzed the broader enclothed-cognition literature with z-curve and meta-analytic methods. Their paper appeared online in 2023 and in a 2025 journal issue.

Their conclusion is more qualified than either extreme. The literature contains evidence consistent with some enclothed-cognition effects. But the earliest studies showed weaker replicability. The work does not give us a universal size or a promise that any meaningful garment will produce a reliable outcome in everyday life.

**ON SCREEN — EVIDENCE CARD C03**
BEST CURRENT LABEL: qualified.
WHY: pooled evidence across heterogeneous garments, meanings, tasks and outcomes.
DOES NOT PROVE: every garment or every context produces an effect.
SOURCE: doi.org/10.1177/01461672231182478

## 8:15–10:20 — The other nervous systems in the room

**IMAGE**
Hallé, *Costume Armor and Sword in the Classical Style*, ca. 1788–90. The Met. Public Domain.

**VOICEOVER**
Clothes can also work through the people looking at them.

In a 2014 experiment, Michael Kraus and Wendy Berry Mendes assigned male participants to clothing that signaled higher class—a business suit—or lower class—sweatpants—before a negotiation with another participant who did not know the clothing was assigned. They reported differences in negotiation behavior and physiological measures for the wearers, and vigilance-related physiological responses in the people facing them.

This was not a brain study. It involved male dyads, culturally specific class signals, and a modified negotiation task. It cannot tell us that a suit universally causes dominance. But it shows why clothing is not a private input. An outfit changes what another person predicts about you. Their response becomes part of your environment.

**ON SCREEN — EVIDENCE CARD C04**
SUPPORTED, BOUNDED: attire operated as a status cue in one dyadic experiment.
MEASURED: negotiation behavior, hormones and cardiovascular physiology.
NOT MEASURED: neural activity or a universal formalwear effect.
SOURCE: doi.org/10.1037/xge0000023

## 10:20–11:45 — The evidence-fitted answer

**RN TO CAMERA**
So: can clothes change how you think?

Sometimes clothing may contribute to changes in attention, feeling or conduct. But “clothing” is not one intervention. A garment has felt properties, learned meanings and social consequences. An effect can depend on the person, the task, the culture, the observer and the situation.

The iconic lab-coat Stroop result did not directly replicate. So do not use it as proof that your blazer raises your IQ or that fashion rewires the brain.

Here is the better model: clothing is part of a body–meaning–situation system. Ask which part changed. Ask what was measured. Ask whether the result replicated. Then fit the sentence to the evidence.
