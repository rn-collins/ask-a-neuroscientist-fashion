# AAN-F-006 — Why Are Runway Soundtracks So Powerful?

## Recording master · 12–14 minutes

### Cold open — 0:00–0:45

[Picture: the same BFC runway entrance twice: first muted, then with the authorized embedded audio. Do not extract or reuse the track outside the embed.]

**RN:** Mute a runway and the clothes still move. Turn the sound back on and the entrance may suddenly feel inevitable. The soundtrack did not enter the garment. It changed the time, expectation and associations in which you encountered it.

On-screen boundary: **Powerful ≠ universal. Entrainment ≠ mind control.**

### Act I — The ear builds a room · 0:45–3:00

**RN:** A live show is an acoustic mixture: music, footsteps, applause, voices, ventilation and reflections. Auditory scene analysis is the name for how listeners group mixtures into perceptual sources. What becomes foreground depends on acoustics, attention and prior knowledge. A livestream rebuilds that scene through microphones, mixing, compression and your playback device. It cannot tell us what one person heard in one seat.

[Visual: Trainor review title and source; then BFC record. Alt narration: “The screen alternates between the source record and a runway image.”]

### Act II — A beat is a forecast · 3:00–5:30

**RN:** Beat perception recruits auditory and motor systems. A pulse offers a temporal prediction: another event is likely around here. People may tap, sway, walk or simply anticipate. Laboratory synchronization and rehabilitation findings show that rhythmic cues can support movement timing. They do not show that a creative director can remotely control a model’s gait.

[Object: Maelzel metronome, The Met, Public Domain.]

**RN:** A metronome is a useful object and a dangerous metaphor. Runway movement is negotiated among the model, shoes, garment, choreography, floor, rehearsal and sound—not reduced to one pendulum.

### Act III — Expectation makes drama · 5:30–8:15

**RN:** Musical systems teach listeners what continuations are probable. Delay, repetition, arrival and violation can redirect attention and shape tension or pleasure. But expectations are learned. Familiarity, training and culture matter. There is no universal drop, tempo or bass line that guarantees awe.

[Visual: Di Liberto et al. natural-music EEG paper; no paper figures reproduced.]

### Act IV — Sound edits the image · 8:15–10:30

**RN:** Runway sound is audiovisual. Temporal synchrony and content fit can change how streams are integrated. The same silhouette may seem severe, playful or ceremonial beside different music, but that is a situated interpretation—not a property neuroscience discovers inside the dress.

Music can also cue autobiographical memory. A familiar track may retrieve a period, person or place. That memory is reconstructive and personal. Another listener may bring no memory at all.

### Act V — Fit the claim · 10:30–12:30

**RN:** Volume matters, but a published video cannot recover in-room sound dose. Duration, distance, spectrum, acoustics, control and hearing all matter. If you want to understand one listening, map it: What was audible? What did the image do? What did your body do? What memory appeared? What remains unknown?

**RN:** A runway soundtrack can organize time, attention, movement and memory without controlling a mind. Watch next: *What Does Fashion Week Do to the Nervous System?*
