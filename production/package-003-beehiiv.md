# Your skin does not read the fiber label

**Subject A:** Why one fabric feels like relief—and another like an alarm  
**Subject B:** “Sensory-friendly” is not a scientific property  
**Preview:** Comfort is an encounter among textile, garment, body and world.

The word *soft* looks factual on a product page. It is not. A textile can be soft under a hand and unbearable at a neck seam. Cotton can flutter, scrape, trap heat or settle. Silk can glide or cling. The fiber name leaves out the garment.

Touch begins with deformation of skin. Fast Aβ afferents help encode location, pressure, vibration and texture. C-tactile afferents contribute to affective qualities of gentle moving touch on hairy skin. Itch, pain and temperature have overlapping but distinct pathways. Clothing recruits these systems through pressure, stretch, friction, heat and repetition.

But laboratory touch is not a whole day in clothes. A 2024 textile study found that measured tactile sensitivity influenced texture ratings; its hand-touch task did not include seams, sweat, movement or social demand. A study of ten autistic adults documented both serious clothing distress and positive sensory seeking. Its lesson is variability, not an “autism wardrobe.”

That matters because marketing turns uncertainty into promises. Compression garments are sold as calming, but small studies do not establish universal therapeutic effects. “Natural” and “sensory-friendly” are not standardized guarantees. A person’s preference is useful; a product category is not a diagnosis.

Try a more precise question: what happens at the surface, seam, pressure, temperature, moisture, movement, body site, duration and context? Change one condition at a time. Document this garment on this day. Respect the result without turning it into a rule for everyone else.
