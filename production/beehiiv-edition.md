# Beehiiv edition — The Study Everyone Quotes

**Subject:** The lab coat did not make anyone smarter  
**Preview:** What the famous clothing-and-cognition study measured—and what its replication changed.

## Can clothes actually change how you think?

The phrase *enclothed cognition* is almost engineered to travel. It suggests that clothing is software for the self: choose the correct symbol, put it on the body, activate the corresponding mind.

The 2012 study underneath the phrase was narrower. Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky reported that participants wearing a white lab coat made fewer errors on incongruent trials in a Stroop task. Later experiments varied the coat’s description and whether participants wore or merely saw it. The authors proposed that a garment’s symbolic meaning and the physical experience of wearing it worked together.

The experiment did not measure intelligence. It did not measure creativity, confidence or long-term change. It did not image the brain.

Then, in 2019, Devin Burns, Elizabeth Fox, Michael Greenstein, Gayla Olbricht and DeMaris Montgomery published a preregistered direct replication. They did not recover the original Stroop effect. Their equivalence analysis suggested that, if the effect exists, it was too small for the original design to detect reliably.

That does not prove that clothing never influences a wearer. It changes the label on the famous result: from accepted fact to disputed finding.

A later evidence synthesis by C. Blaine Horton Jr., Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky found a more qualified pattern across the broader literature. Some effects appear to have evidential value; the earliest work was less replicable. But pooled evidence across different garments, meanings and outcomes cannot guarantee what one outfit will do to one person in daily life.

The most useful model is not “clothes change the brain.” It is **body + meaning + situation**.

- **Body:** pressure, temperature, movement, proprioception and comfort.
- **Meaning:** role, memory, identity and learned association.
- **Situation:** observers, institutions, tasks and consequences.

An outfit may change any of these without changing all of them. The question is never simply whether clothing “works.” It is what changed, for whom, in which room, compared with what.
